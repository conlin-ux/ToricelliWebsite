From Bernoulli to Torricelli

A step-by-step derivation of the exit velocity, the continuity relation we use to measure it, and the quasi-steady drain model that gives our predicted h(t) and drain times.

01 Problem setup

We apply Bernoulli's equation along a streamline from point 1, the free surface in the column, to point 2, the jet just outside the orifice (Figure 2). The height h is measured from the orifice centerline up to the free surface. Both points are open to the atmosphere.

The column has inner diameter D₁ = 2.625 in. Two orifices were tested, D₂ = 1/8 in and 3/16 in. Table 2 lists the resulting areas; the area ratio A₁/A₂ = (D₁/D₂)² sets how fast the surface falls relative to the jet.

02 Bernoulli's equation → Torricelli's law

We start from the head form of Bernoulli's equation between points 1 and 2 (de Nevers and Silcox 2021), where Δ means (point 2) − (point 1):

$$\Delta\left(\frac{P}{\rho g} + z + \frac{v^2}{2g}\right) = \frac{dW_{n.f.}}{g\,dm} - \frac{\mathfrak{F}}{g}$$
(1)

Here \(dW_{n.f.}/dm\) is non-flow (shaft) work per unit mass and \(\mathfrak{F}\) is friction heating per unit mass. We apply the assumptions one at a time:

  1. Incompressible, quasi-steady flow. Water density is constant, and the flow at each instant is treated as steady (justified in Section 05), so Eq. 1 holds at every moment of the drain.
  2. No shaft work. The pump is off (0 % power) during draining, so \(dW_{n.f.}/dm = 0\).
  3. Frictionless orifice. Friction and vena-contracta losses are neglected: \(\mathfrak{F} = 0\).
  4. Equal pressures. The column top is open and the jet discharges to air, so \(P_1 = P_2 = P_{atm}\) and the pressure term vanishes.
  5. Datum at the orifice centerline. \(z_2 = 0\) and \(z_1 = h\), so \(z_2 - z_1 = -h\).

With these, Eq. 1 reduces to

$$-h + \frac{v_2^2 - v_1^2}{2g} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_2^2 - v_1^2 = 2gh$$
(1a)

Continuity (Eq. 3 below) gives \(v_1 = (A_2/A_1)\,v_2\), so

$$v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1-(A_2/A_1)^2}}$$
(1b)

Negligible column velocity. For our rig, \((v_1/v_2)^2 = (A_2/A_1)^2 \approx 5.1\times10^{-6}\) for the 1/8 in orifice and \(2.6\times10^{-5}\) for the 3/16 in orifice. The denominator in Eq. 1b is 1 to better than 0.003 %, so we drop it and obtain Torricelli's law:

$$v_2 = \sqrt{2gh}$$
(2)

This is the speed a body reaches falling freely through height h, and it does not depend on the orifice size. The same result, with the same assumptions, is derived in open-access form by Halpern (2024).

Units note

We work in inches throughout. With h in in and g = 32.174 ft/s² × 12 = 386.1 in/s², Eq. 2 gives v₂ directly in in/s, the unit used in our velocity plots. The conversion factor gc = 32.174 lbm·ft/(lbf·s²) is only needed when pressure terms are written in lbf; because P₁ = P₂ = Patm, those terms cancel and gc drops out of Torricelli's law.

03 Continuity: measuring the exit velocity

The ultrasonic sensor measures only the water level, so we cannot see v₂ directly. Mass conservation links the jet to the falling surface. For an incompressible fluid,

$$\rho A_1 v_1 = \rho A_2 v_2, \qquad v_1 = -\frac{dh}{dt}$$
(3)

so the experimental exit velocity follows from the measured slope of h(t):

$$v_{2,\text{exp}} = \frac{A_1}{A_2}\left(-\frac{dh}{dt}\right)$$
(4)

Because A₁/A₂ is 441 (1/8 in) or 196 (3/16 in), any error in dh/dt is multiplied by several hundred. We therefore compute dh/dt with a centered 5-point running (local linear-fit) derivative of the logged data, not by differentiating a fitted curve; see Data & Experiment.

04 Falling-head (quasi-steady) drain model

Substituting Torricelli's law (Eq. 2) into continuity (Eq. 3) gives a differential equation for the water level. We include a discharge coefficient Cd to show where real losses would enter; ideal theory has Cd = 1:

$$A_1\frac{dh}{dt} = -C_d A_2\sqrt{2gh}$$
(5)

Separating variables and integrating from h₀ at t = 0:

$$\begin{aligned}\int_{h_0}^{h}\frac{dh}{\sqrt{h}} &= -C_d\frac{A_2}{A_1}\sqrt{2g}\int_0^t dt\\[4pt] \Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt{h} &= \sqrt{h_0} - C_d\frac{A_2}{A_1}\sqrt{\frac{g}{2}}\,t\end{aligned}$$
(6)

so √h falls linearly with time and h(t) is a parabola:

$$h(t) = \left(\sqrt{h_0} - C_d\frac{A_2}{A_1}\sqrt{\frac{g}{2}}\,t\right)^2$$
(7)

Setting h = hf gives the time to drain from h₀ to hf:

$$t_{\text{drain}} = \frac{A_1}{C_d A_2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{g}}\left(\sqrt{h_0}-\sqrt{h_f}\right)$$
(8)

Eq. 7 with Cd = 1 is the dashed "Theoretical" curve on our height plots, and Eq. 2 evaluated along it is the dashed line on our velocity plots. Eq. 8 with hf = 0 gives our theoretical drain times. In a real orifice, friction and the contraction of the jet just downstream of the hole (the vena contracta) make Cd < 1, so real orifices pass less flow than the ideal model predicts. We use Cd only as a concept when discussing our results; we do not report a numerical value.

05 Is the quasi-steady assumption justified?

Bernoulli's equation in the form of Eq. 1 is for steady flow, but our level falls continuously. The approximation is acceptable if the jet speed changes slowly compared with gravitational acceleration. Differentiating Eq. 2 and using \(dh/dt = -(A_2/A_1)\,v_2\):

$$\frac{dv_2}{dt} = \frac{\sqrt{2g}}{2\sqrt{h}}\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{g}{v_2}\left(-\frac{A_2}{A_1}v_2\right) = -g\,\frac{A_2}{A_1}$$
(9)

The jet decelerates at a constant rate, which is why the theoretical velocity lines on our plots are straight.

07 Pre-lab questions

Our answers from the pre-lab meeting. The Conclusions compare them with what we measured.

Q1 What are the safety considerations for this experiment?
  • Wear safety glasses, long pants and closed-toe shoes in the lab.
  • Secure the column, tubing and orifice flange before filling. Tighten the flange bolts fully so the column doesn't leak.
  • Don't overfill the column. Stop the pump (0 % power) once the target height is reached.
  • Take care when swapping orifice flanges; the column must be empty to avoid spray.
  • Keep water away from power outlets, the electronics box and the computer.
  • Wipe up spills promptly to prevent slips.
  • Never leave the equipment running unattended, and send only pure water down the drain (Torricelli SOP).
Q2 Starting from Bernoulli's equation, what assumptions give Torricelli's law?

Starting from Bernoulli's equation between the free surface (1) and the orifice exit (2):

$$\frac{P_1}{\rho}+\frac{v_1^2}{2}+gz_1=\frac{P_2}{\rho}+\frac{v_2^2}{2}+gz_2$$

Assumptions:

  1. Steady (quasi-steady) and incompressible flow.
  2. No pump, turbine or other shaft work.
  3. Frictionless orifice (no friction losses).
  4. \(P_1 = P_2 = P_{atm}\): the column is open to the atmosphere and the jet discharges to the atmosphere.
  5. \(v_1 \approx 0\): the column area is much larger than the orifice area, so the surface falls slowly.
  6. Datum at the orifice centerline: \(z_2 = 0\), \(z_1 = h\).

The equation then reduces to \(gh = \tfrac{1}{2}v_2^2\), so

$$v_2=\sqrt{2gh}\quad\text{(Torricelli's law)}$$

The exit velocity is then linked to the falling surface by mass conservation: \((\rho A v)_1 = (\rho A v)_2 \Rightarrow v_2 = A_1 v_1 / A_2\).

Q3 Would different pipe (column) diameters affect the speed or drain time of the fluid leaving through the orifice? In theory? In reality?

In theory: since \(v_2=\sqrt{2gh}\), the exit velocity at any given height is the same regardless of column diameter. The column diameter does control how fast h falls (\(dh/dt = -(A_2/A_1)v_2\)). A narrower column drains faster, so its exit velocity drops off sooner. A wider column takes longer to drain.

In reality: friction, turbulence and vena-contracta effects make the actual exit velocity lower than the ideal value, so measured results won't match exactly.

Q4 Based on Eq. 2, how does the orifice size change the speed of the flow?

Orifice diameter does not appear in \(v_2=\sqrt{2gh}\), so ideally the exit velocity at a given height doesn't depend on orifice size. The volumetric flow rate \(Q = A_2 v_2\) is higher for a larger orifice, so the column drains faster. Drain time scales as \(1/A_2\), so the 3/16 in orifice should drain \((3/16 \div 1/8)^2 = 2.25\times\) faster than the 1/8 in orifice. In reality, losses can differ between orifice sizes, so the measured velocities may not be identical.

Q5 What is your plan to complete the experiment?

Install the first orifice (1/8 in) and run draining tests from 18, 22 and 27 in, with four trials at each height. Then swap to the second orifice (3/16 in) and repeat, for 24 runs in total. Log h(t) with the ultrasonic sensor, then calculate experimental exit velocities from continuity and compare them, and the drain times, with Torricelli's predictions.
